Cognitive assessment instrument · build complete

Do you remember what, or do you remember who?

Alzheimer's disease damages the binding between a memory and its context before it damages the memory itself. You keep the fact and lose its source. This instrument is built to measure that gap directly — the distance between remembering that a delivery was short, and remembering who told you.

Not validated

No human being has taken this test. Everything measured below is a property of a pipeline answering itself: a generator that writes stories, a question engine, simulated participants, and an analysis. The analysis is shown to be correct. The instrument is not shown to measure memory.

That distinction is the whole point of this page, and every number on it carries the source it came from.

Two parts

Part A is the long form, the first prototype, kept exactly as it stood. Part B is the current work: the short form, one story of about 3.7 minutes, then 10 to 20 questions, in Egyptian Arabic, built to compare groups — healthy older adults, normal ageing, mild cognitive impairment, Alzheimer's disease — and not yet to score one person. The appendix at the end explains every term and abbreviation. Like everything here, the results come from simulation and claim nothing beyond it.

A.1 · The premise

One story, two memories, and the difference between them

A participant hears a short workplace narrative. Afterwards they are asked two kinds of question, and the instrument only cares about the gap.

Content

“Was there a problem with the delivery?”

What happened. The fact itself, stripped of its surroundings. In early Alzheimer's this holds up comparatively well — it can be answered from a feeling of familiarity.

−

Context

“Who mentioned it?”

Who, when, why, and what it corrected. The bindings between facts. These cannot be answered by familiarity; they need the relation itself to have survived.

D = mean(relational accuracy) − content accuracy. A difference, never a ratio.

The ratio form was the first thing this build refused. Dividing rather than subtracting costs about 0.2 of reliability outright, and worse, it manufactures the exact signature the study is looking for: as content accuracy falls with age, a ratio rises on its own, with no change in binding whatsoever. The analysis now rejects a ratio-shaped measure in code, so it cannot be reintroduced by accident.

Four kinds of context are probed separately, because they can dissociate from each other and a single averaged score would hide that.

A.2 · Take the test

The instrument, running

This is not a mock-up. The story below came out of the graph generator, the questions out of the probe engine with their real distractor categories, and the scoring is the same content-versus-context split the analysis uses. Only the words are placeholder — the real study runs in Egyptian Arabic, narrated aloud over pictures.

The story seed 7 · 47 elements

The short form is 10 questions; the full form runs every question this one story can support, 39 of them. A real session uses 162 across two different stories, so even the full form here is roughly a third of the instrument. It exists to make the premise legible in two minutes, not to measure the person clicking.

A.3 · The numbers

What has actually been shown

The analysis was fed data whose truth was known in advance, in three worlds. Two of them contain no effect at all — content ability and context ability are literally the same variable — and one contains a real separation of 0.6.

Where the effect is real, the reliability of the difference score reaches 0.720 against a threshold of 0.70 — and in the null worlds it does not, which is what makes the threshold a criterion rather than a formality.

The number to weigh is the last one. Asked to recover a correlation of 0.6, the analysis returns 0.635; asked where the two abilities are one variable, it returns 0.973. That says the chain from generator through questions through responses through analysis is wired correctly end to end. It says nothing about whether the task measures memory.

More participants cannot buy reliability

Rate of reaching PROCEED, by true separability and sample size. Each line is one true ρ. Flat lines mean sample size is not the lever.

The effective choice set, measured

Four options are offered. A classifier restricted to what a participant can actually perceive says the real number is fewer. The design intends 3; the dashed line is where it lands.

The left chart is the counter-intuitive result that set the length of the test. The reliability of a difference score is a property of how many questions you ask, not how many people you ask. At a true separability of 0.7, going from 100 participants to 500 does not move the success rate at all. If the instrument comes back too short, the fix is more questions, not more people.

The right chart is the most important measurement in the project, and the reason the question count rose. The lowest measured effective choice set is 1.83 — a participant is really choosing between two options, not four — which raises the guess floor and costs reliability. The sizing consumes these measured values instead of the 3 the design assumed.

A.4 · How it works

A graph, a story, and four ways to be wrong

Every narrative is generated from a graph, so the correct answer to every question is a fact about the structure rather than a judgement about the prose.

One narrative is 47 elements and 38 relations across 9 named people and 3 scenes, told in about 746 words. A session is two of them: 94 elements, 18 names, 1492 words. Standard clinical story-recall tasks run 65–180 words, and whether an older adult can hold this is the single most likely reason the design fails.

The 162 questions divide as 70 content questions and 9 of each relational kind per story, an allocation found by simulation rather than chosen: it is the shortest test that still measures the gap reliably under the worst-case guessing assumption.

Scenes are an orthogonal dimension, deliberately. If topic and structure were correlated, a wrong answer could be explained by topical similarity rather than by a broken binding, and the error analysis would be worthless. Keeping them independent is checked, not assumed.

The anatomy of one question

Every relational question offers four options and an “I don't remember” button that counts as a real answer. The options are not decoration: which wrong one a participant picks is itself a measurement.

23 automatic checks

Every generated story and question set is checked before it can be used. The colour of each left edge is the thing worth noticing: the first sixteen checks examine the graph, and the last seven examine the prose. For a long time only the first group existed, and a defect that lived entirely in the wording survived all of them.

Check 16 is worth reading twice. It permits a small number of questions to be dropped when the graph cannot supply a clean set of options. Over 400 generated stories that is 1.53% of all questions — which sounds like rounding error — but TEMPORAL_NEXT is the only family that can drop at all, so the rate that describes what actually happens is 8.29% within that family. Both are reported, family first.

A.5 · What broke

Eight times this project produced a clean, ordered, plausible, wrong answer

None of them crashed. That is the entire problem: a measurement instrument fails by returning a number, not by throwing an error. What follows is the honest list, because a collaborator's first question is what else might still be hiding.

The first two were found by someone outside the project, in a single pass, after eight rounds of our own adversarial review. They are listed first because they are the most damaging — and because a process failing is more worth knowing about than a process working.

Q34

The main criterion could have passed on an artefact — and we did not find it

found from outside

What it reported

A two-factor model preferred in every simulated cohort where the effect was real, and in none where it was absent. Exactly the result the design was built to produce.

What was true

The two kinds of question differed in how they were asked as well as in what they asked about. Content used wrong answers that never occurred, so a vague sense of familiarity could solve them; context used wrong answers that did occur, so it could not. A two-factor result was therefore equally well explained by recognition versus recall.

Why it survivedEight rounds of our own adversarial review, five standing rules, hundreds of tests, and a public page listing six of our own failures. Our v2 review even saw the asymmetry — and called it a bias pointing toward the hypothesis, then prescribed measuring it. That was the wrong prescription for the right observation: the problem was never the size of the bias, it was that two explanations fit the same data equally well. No amount of measuring tells you which one you have.

The fixContent questions now use wrong answers that did occur, with the same option geometry as the context questions. Measured effective choice set 2.59, inside the relational range, and the only family whose wording gives nothing away. A smaller block of the old form is kept so the difference between the two ways of asking can be measured rather than assumed absent, at 20 questions.

What it costThe test went from 112 questions to 162, and the session from 27 minutes to 34.5 against a 35-minute ceiling. The requirement had been written down before it was missed: the theoretical brief this project came from asks for a battery that taxes content and context with matched difficulty.

Q35

The retest simulation contained no retest

found from outside

What it reported

A reliability of 0.754, comfortably above the 0.70 threshold — and the number that had set the length of the test across four revisions.

What was true

Both sessions were the same simulated person with the same abilities. Only the coin flips differed. So the figure measured how many questions had been asked and nothing else: an upper bound, presented as an estimate.

Why it survivedThe two sessions genuinely differed, so every check that compared them passed. Nothing asked whether they differed in the way a real fortnight differs.

The fixReal occasion and form variance — and the result is better than anyone expected. Day-to-day variation costs the gap nothing: it moves both halves of a within-person difference together and cancels out exactly. What does not cancel is a difference in difficulty between the two stories, which lands on the gap twice over. So reliability is bounded by how well the two stories are matched, not by how variable people are from one day to the next.

What it costAt the honest retest the old length no longer reached the threshold: power fell to 0.403 before re-sizing, and the shortfall routed correctly to lengthen the test rather than stop the programme, which is exactly what the third outcome exists for.

Q9

The measure manufactured its own result

critical

What it reported

A clean rise in the context/content ratio with age, exactly the signature the study was designed to find.

What was true

A ratio rises whenever its denominator falls. Content accuracy falls with age on its own, so the signature appeared with no change in binding at all.

Why it survivedThe specification named the measure as a ratio and every test checked that the ratio was computed correctly. Nothing asked whether it should be one.

The fixD became a difference. The analysis now refuses a ratio-shaped measure in code. Cost: none — the difference is also about 0.2 more reliable.

v5

The confidence measure was calibrated from confidence

critical

What it reported

An over-confidence index that behaved perfectly across the whole simulated range, tracking miscalibration exactly as intended.

What was true

The simulator drew confidence from the same parameter the index was recovering. It could not have failed, and so its success meant nothing.

Why it survivedBecause it passed. A test that cannot fail looks exactly like a test that passes.

The fixA deliberate miscalibration parameter was added so the simulated data could disagree with the model, and the index was re-validated against data it had not been fitted to.

Q28

One wrong answer was never mentioned in the story

critical

What it reported

375 tests passing, every distractor category correctly populated, orthogonality within tolerance.

What was true

For two of the four question types, the semantic lure never appeared in the narrative. A participant could eliminate it as “the name I never heard” without remembering anything.

Why it survivedEvery check ran on the graph. The defect was in the prose, and until this point nothing checked the prose at all.

The fixA narration layer with its own checks: every node must be mentioned, every option must have a referent, no correction may point forward.

Q30

The lure was mentioned — but never allowed to speak

major

What it reported

Check 17 green: every node narrated. The Q28 fix verified.

What was true

One person appeared three times and never said a word while every other option spoke. One decision was the only one without a “because of that”. Eliminable in three seconds.

Why it survivedThe fix satisfied the letter of the check. Being mentioned and being mentioned in the same role are different properties, and only the first had been written down.

The fixRole parity: every person speaks, every decision carries a reason, every claim a follow-up. What varies between nodes is now what they are attached to in the graph, never how they appear on the page.

Q31

The leak detector was calibrated to pass the leak

critical

What it reported

“At floor, pass.” The classifier built to catch a giveaway option reported that the wording gave nothing away.

What was true

It was scoring against a floor of 1/3 — a number derived from the design's own intention to make one option eliminable. So “eliminated exactly one option” read as success, and it passed a question a human eliminates by reading it.

Why it survivedThe floor came from the thing being judged. A measurement calibrated from its own subject cannot detect that subject — it can only confirm it.

The fixEverything now scores against 1/4, the only baseline that assumes nothing, and the effective choice set is measured per family rather than assumed. Cost: the true figures (2.04, 1.91, 2.03, 1.83) went back into the sizing and raised the test from 104 questions to 162.

Q31b

The same error, inside the fix for the first one

major

What it reported

A corrected leakage measurement, scored honestly against 1/4.

What was true

One of its features, ever_speaks, was being computed from the graph. The measurement meant to contain only what a participant can perceive was reading the very relation under test.

Why it survivedIt was found the same day, by re-reading the fix with the lesson from the bug it was fixing. Nothing automatic caught it.

The fixThe feature now reads from the narration. Perceivable means perceivable in the artefact that actually ships.

D11

A check that tested the wrong string, and so could not fail

notable

What it reported

Green. Every document's stated word count verified against the code.

What was true

It was searching for the budget figure, not the claimed count. The documents said 741 words — one seed's value written as a constant — while the true figure was a distribution.

Why it survivedRoughly four minutes. It was written, it passed, and the pass looked wrong for the size of the claim.

The fixThe count is now stated as 746 on average across 40 seeds (735–753), and the assertion is on the worst case against the 774-word budget, because the burden argument is about the story a participant actually gets.

The four standing rules

Each was written after one of the failures above, and each stays in the repository permanently.

  1. Any validation that recovers a signal must also be run where the signal is known to be absent, and shown to recover nothing.

    Written after a pipeline reported a clean effect in a world built to contain none.

  2. Before any validation run, the null world must produce zero effect on the measure being validated — zero to a stated tolerance, not “small”.

    “Small” is a word that survives any number.

  3. Every estimate must carry a check that the model could have produced the observed data at all, not only that the estimate is precise. Precision conditional on a false model is worse than no estimate, because it looks like an answer.

    Written after a parameter was recovered to three decimals by a model that could not have generated the data.

  4. A measurement calibrated from the thing it measures cannot detect that thing.

    Written after Q31, and then immediately needed twice more — for the feature read from the graph, and for the documentation check that searched for the wrong string.

  5. Internal review has a ceiling, and it is lower than it feels.

    Everything else on this page was caught here by running something — a simulation, a classifier, a null world. The two worst entries were caught by an outside reader who read the design and asked what else could produce the same result. Those are different faculties, and more of the first does not buy the second. An outside read is now scheduled before the feasibility study and again before publication.

Each of the five now has a test that fails when the rule is broken. The first four were written down, and then violated anyway — one of them inside the module written to enforce another.

A.6 · Still open

Six things no amount of code can close

Each is written into the pre-registration as a named limitation rather than left to be discovered by a reviewer.

Nobody has taken the test

Every quantitative property on this page is conditional on an invented model of how people answer. The recovery check — asked for 0.6, returns 0.635 — proves the machinery, not the model.

closed only by study A1

Content questions are easier in the wrong direction

Content distractors never happened, so they yield to familiarity — which is relatively preserved in early Alzheimer's. That biases the gap toward the hypothesis. Content foils cannot be drawn from the graph without being true.

A0 familiarity block

Familiarity cannot be equalised structurally

For three of the four node types, total connectedness and relation-specific connectedness are the same number, so the confound cannot be balanced away in the graph.

A0 familiarity block

The order lure announces itself

Its semantic lure is an event with no place in the sequence, and a thing outside a sequence is perceptibly outside it. Giving it a position makes the question ambiguous instead, which is worse.

excluded from the primary analysis

The correction lure is definitional

A semantic lure for “what did this correct?” is a claim never described as corrected — and the correction has to be narrated for the question to be answerable at all. Removing the signal removes the category.

quantified, not removed

The story may be too much to hold

94 elements and 18 named people against a clinical norm of 65–180 words. If an older adult cannot manage it, nothing else matters.

A0, decision rule pre-fixed

Three outcomes, not two

The original plan had a single kill criterion. Splitting it was one of the more consequential corrections in the build, because the two failures it conflated call for opposite responses.

Stop

Once measurement error is accounted for, content and context turn out to be the same thing. The construct is not there. The programme ends and the null result is published.

disattenuated r ≥ 0.80

Lengthen

The construct is there but the test is too short to measure it reliably. This is not a stop — it means add questions and repeat. Collapsing it into “stop” would have killed a live programme for a fixable reason.

ICC of age-residualised D < 0.70

Proceed

Both criteria pass and a two-factor model is preferred over one. Only then does the programme continue to Stage B.

all three, conjunctively

A feasibility study of about 30 people comes first, and its decision rules are already fixed in writing: if dropout among the over-60s exceeds 15%, or accuracy in the last quarter of the questions falls more than 8 points below the first, the test is cut to one story and the reduced reliability is reported as a finding rather than hidden. Cutting to one story is a costed outcome, not a failure.

Part B

Current work: the short form

Chapters B.1 to B.5: what the short form is, what the simulations say about it, the problems found in it and what was done about each, the plan for the clinicians' ideas, and every document behind it.

B.1 · The short form

One story, a few questions, and the same gap

The long form took too long for a memory clinic. The short form keeps its idea and cuts it down: one spoken story of about 3.7 minutes, then 10 to 20 questions, half about what happened and half about its context, in Egyptian Arabic.

Who it is for

Comparing groups: healthy older adults, normal ageing, MCI and AD. The clinicians' starting point is that ageing lowers content and context memory together, while Alzheimer's disease lowers context more. The short form is not yet able to place one person.

How it is scored

Every person gets D, context accuracy minus content accuracy, the easy-to-read score. Groups are compared with the model-based test, which reads each person's two abilities below the percent-correct scale. That test is primary for comparing groups (Q47).

What is fixed, and what is not

The Egyptian Arabic wording goes through the clinicians' Delphi panel. The story will be one human recording by a native speaker, so everyone hears the same voice. No person has taken the short form; every result below is simulation.

B.2 · What it shows

What the simulations say, and what they cannot

Every figure here comes from simulated people answering under an assumed model. The full tables, with their uncertainty, are in the documents in B.5.

Groups: yes, for large differences

With 100 people a group, the model-based test finds a context deficit of half a standard deviation in 0.48 of studies. Where two groups differ only by an equal decline in both memories, it wrongly says "different" in 0.06; D does so in 0.12. Both should be near one in twenty.

One person: not yet

A person's gap measured on two different stories agrees with a reliability of only 0.29 at twenty questions. And there is a ceiling no test length fixes: even measured perfectly, one person's gap at one moment tells MCI from normal ageing with an AUC of 0.654, and AD from normal ageing with 0.736. Healthy people simply differ more by nature than the illness effect that was assumed.

More information from each answer

A right-or-wrong score throws most of an answer away. Conflict questions, where the familiar wrong answer is the tempting one, make a twenty-question form worth 24.6 of today's questions for a healthy older adult when half the questions are conflict questions.

Spoken answers, or three options on screen

With no options on the screen, lucky guesses become much less common: about one blind answer in six comes out right, against one in two today (six is assumed, from the people named in the story). One person's gap then tells MCI from normal ageing with an AUC of 0.590 when one answer in ten is misheard (0.600 when heard perfectly), against 0.570 today, and the bias for weaker people (B.3) falls to 0.000. Three believable options on every question, on screen, do as well (0.587) and remove that bias too. Answer times and the finger's path add little.

Patchy or dimmer: groups only

Does an illness break some kinds of link more than others, where ageing only dims them all? Two tests read each group's own level, so being weaker is not taken for being patchy. A group with ageing's even decline plus an extra loss on "who" (of a size we assumed) is found in 98% of studies of 300 people a group. Unevenness that differs from person to person is found in only 21%. Neither works for one person.

The chosen form, combined

Decided: three believable options on the touch screen, with conflict questions, answer times and the finger's path. Hesitation is left out: it adds little and can fake link failure. With all of these together, one person's gap tells MCI from normal ageing with an AUC of 0.601: 66% of the way from chance to the ceiling above. Spoken answers do about as well (0.599) but need a speech-to-text engine. That comparison holds at the error rates we assumed: one spoken answer in ten misheard, and no stray taps on touch.

Then one group was made slower and more variable, with missed sentences, and tremor and taps on touch or mishearing when spoken, all at once. For an equal decline, three options said "different" in 0.030 and 0.045 of studies (a decline like ageing's, and a far larger one), near the one in twenty chance gives; spoken answers in 0.070 and 0.045. Not in this run: questions that differ in sharpness, a scorer that must learn each question from healthy people (B.3), and examiners. One person still cannot be placed from one test.

A delay: it depends on forgetting nobody has measured

Asked a day later, the ceiling rises to 0.829 under the forgetting we assumed. If the extra forgetting happens only in the first ten minutes (Hart 1988), it is 0.683; if only after days (Weston 2018), 0.654 at a day. So no delay is recommended until real delayed data exist.

B.3 · Problems found

What was wrong, and what was done about it

Found by our own checks, and by six outside checkers who attacked both the literature and this code. Each is in the plan (B.4) with its status.

D moves when everything moves

D is a gap in percent correct, and questions near the top or bottom of their range move less, so an equal decline can look like a gap. If context questions separate people less sharply than content questions, D says "different" in 0.28 of studies of an equal decline. No scoring fixes that. Decided: the model-based test is primary for comparing groups, and D stays as the easy-to-read score.

How sharply questions separate people

A scorer that assumes every question is equally sharp read an equal decline as link failure in 0.16 of studies when context questions were sharper. Calibrated on healthy people, and comparing declines in units of healthy people's spread, it reads 0.065; over all three cases of sharpness it averages 0.073, still a little above one in twenty. Mostly fixed in the model-based test.

Weaker people looked better than they are

For people well below average on both memories, a person's gap read 0.089 too high, because context questions have fewer believable wrong answers, so lucky guesses prop them up. With equally believable options it reads 0.001. The fix is in the questions: better wrong answers for context questions, through the Delphi, or spoken answers with no options at all.

Forgetting, and two sessions

The forgetting we assumed is contradicted by the two studies cited for it: once learning was matched, Alzheimer's patients forgot at the normal rate (Kopelman 1985). Delay results are now shown under four versions. Testing twice, now and later, is withdrawn: one story has too few facts for two different forms.

Examiners who read the story differently

When patients and controls are tested by different examiners, and examiners read the links in the story differently from the facts, the test says "link failure" in 0.33 of studies of an equal decline. One recorded voice reads 0.065. Decided: one human recording.

Hesitation, and missed sentences

A group that simply hesitates more on every answer starts to look like link failure (0.083). Slowness cancels within each person; hesitation does not, and it adds little, so it is left out (decided). Sentences missed while listening fake little link failure at the rates assumed (0.067 when ageing misses one sentence in four).

The wider search

Thirty-two independent searches of the literature, then six checkers. Their shared verdict: no method yet measures this gap for one person, ours included. Everything found so far is a group difference. The most popular idea, reading the order people recall words in, failed its one fair test.

Still open

The questions only the investigators and clinicians can settle are Q43 to Q48, in the open questions document (B.5): how fast Egyptian Arabic is read aloud, whether the long form should be re-sized, and how one person could ever be placed.

B.4 · The plan

The clinicians' ideas, built into the model

Every answer comes from a hidden state: the person remembered the link; had the fact but not the link and was pulled to the familiar wrong answer; guessed; or said "I don't remember". Each idea the clinicians raised is one more clue about that state, and each gets one small piece of code: how likely the clue is in each state.

The steps

  1. Fix our own work first: question sharpness, weaker people, forgetting, the spread across stories. Done.
  2. Spoken answers: no options shown, so lucky guesses are less common. Done: a real gain, level with three options on screen.
  3. Time to answer and hesitations, read within each person. Done: a small gain. Hesitation is left out (decided).
  4. The finger's path on the screen, bending toward the tempting answer. Done: no gain in AUC.
  5. Attention: a missed sentence must not fake a gap. Done: it fakes little.
  6. Other conditions, as patterns across the four context families. Done: a fixed pattern shows in groups; unevenness from person to person hardly shows.
  7. One recorded voice, and trouble with the device. Done: supports one recording. Stray taps cost a little, and taps that grow with age push false alarms up a little.
  8. Combine what survives into one honest number. Done: three options on screen chosen; spoken answers do about as well but need speech-to-text.

An outside review checked the code behind every step before any result was written up. It found four real faults, all fixed: one scorer's assumed rate of missed sentences, one test's spread, a combined run that left out two problems, and tests that could not fail. A second outside reading of the written results found claims the numbers did not back, such as two forms called level without saying the tie rests on assumed error rates. Those are corrected.

Decided by the investigators

  • The model-based test is primary for comparing groups (Q47).
  • Destination memory ("whom did I tell?") is parked for the clinicians.
  • Speech-to-text is simulated only for now; the first study saves the audio, with consent, to judge an engine later.
  • The story is one human recording, made after the Delphi.
  • A test-retest study in healthy older adults is planned, and existing data will be asked for. Participants wait for the clinicians.
  • Answers: three believable options on screen. The Delphi writes two good wrong answers for every question.
  • Hesitation is left out of the scoring.

Next: follow people for years

One test on one day cannot place a person, so the plan is to test people again over the years and look back at who is diagnosed. A simulation of that study, with the gene APOE e4 and published rates of diagnosis, plays on its own page: the follow-up study. Who is diagnosed is drawn from the published rates, never from the test, and every run is checked for a result the code could have planted.

What only real people can tell us

How closely the two memories go together in healthy older adults, and how stable that is; how sharply each question separates people; how strongly a familiar wrong answer pulls; and how accurate speech-to-text is for older Egyptian Arabic speakers.

Appendix

Definitions, abbreviations, and how to check

I · Definitions

The words this page uses

In plain words. A term in a document means the same as here.

Content memory
Memory for what happened: the fact, the object, the thing itself.
Context memory
Memory for the circumstances: who was there, when it was, what caused it, what changed it.
The link
What ties a fact to its context. The idea being tested is that early Alzheimer's disease weakens the link before it weakens memory for the fact.
D
The project's main score: context accuracy minus content accuracy. Below zero means context is remembered worse than content. Kept as the easy-to-read score; groups are compared with the model-based test (Q47).
Gap
The same idea as D on the model's own scale: a person's context ability minus their content ability.
Context family
One of the four kinds of context question: social (who), temporal (when, what came before), causal (why) and revision (what changed).
Long form
The first prototype: two stories and many questions. Kept unchanged as a prototype (Part A).
Short form
The current design: one spoken story, then 10 to 20 questions, in Egyptian Arabic (Part B).
Story bank
All the questions one story can support. Each person answers a different balanced subset of it.
Guessing floor
The share of questions someone gets right by luck, without remembering. It depends on how many answer options are believable.
Effective choice set (n_eff)
How many answer options are really believable to someone who does not remember. Two means a coin toss.
Lure
A wrong answer built to look right.
Conflict question
A question whose wrong answer is tied to the fact more strongly than the right answer is, so someone who remembers the fact but not the link is drawn to it.
Pull
How often someone who has the fact but not the link picks that tempting wrong answer. Assumed; only real answers can measure it.
Slope
How sharply a question separates stronger from weaker memories. Two sets of questions can be equally hard and still differ in slope.
Healthy-SD units
Differences measured in units of how much healthy people differ from each other. An equal decline means the same number of these units on both kinds of memory.
Symmetric decline
Both kinds of memory falling by the same amount, as in normal ageing. A good test must then say: no difference in the gap.
Chapman and Chapman problem
A gap in percent correct can move when everything moves, because questions near the top or bottom of their range move less. Only questions matched in difficulty AND slope avoid it.
Residual method
Context accuracy minus what content accuracy predicts. Dropped: it reads a symmetric decline as a context problem (Q47).
Model-based group test
Compares two groups' average abilities directly, below the percent-correct scale. Primary for comparing groups (Q47).
Calibration
Estimating each question's difficulty, and how much people differ, from the answers of healthy people.
Prior
What the model assumes about a person before seeing their answers. With few answers it still outweighs them.
Information
How much one answer narrows down a person's ability. It adds up over answers.
Ceiling
The best a score could ever do with perfect measurement. It is limited by how much healthy people differ by nature, not by test length.
Null world
A simulated world where content and context are one ability. Any gap found there is an error of the method.
False alarm
Saying two groups differ when they do not. A test at the usual standard should do this 5% of the time (0.05).
Power
How often a test finds a difference that is really there.
Monte Carlo standard error
How much a simulated result would move if the simulation were run again with new random numbers.
Forgetting rate
How fast a memory fades after the story. All rates here are assumed.
Testing effect
Being asked about something makes the memory of it stronger.
REAL, NOISE_FLAT, NOISE_AGE
The long form's three simulated worlds: one where content and context are two different abilities (REAL), and two where they are one ability, with and without an age effect.
Delphi
A structured way for a panel of experts to agree, in rounds. Used for the final Egyptian Arabic wording.
II · Abbreviations

What each abbreviation stands for

The first time an abbreviation appears on this page, and in each document, it links here.

2PL
two-parameter logistic model. An IRT model in which every question has its own difficulty and its own slope.
A0
the feasibility study. The first small study with real people: can people take the test, and do the questions work.
A1
registered analysis 1, construct validity. Do content and context memory behave as two linked but different abilities?
A2
registered analysis 2, reliability. Does a person get the same result when tested twice?
A3
registered analysis 3, age. How the scores change with age. Descriptive only.
AD
Alzheimer's disease. In the simulations, a group whose context memory is assumed to be hit harder than content.
AD8
eight-item informant interview. A short questionnaire a family member answers about changes in memory and thinking.
AUC
area under the curve. How often a person from one group scores beyond a person from the other. 0.5 is a coin toss; 1 is always.
D1 to D11
deliverables. The numbered parts of the build specification, for example D11, the integrity pass.
EAP
expected a posteriori. The model's best single guess about a person after their answers, pulled toward the middle when the answers say little.
FM1 to FM6
failure modes. Numbered ways the specification says the instrument could fail, each with a standing check.
ICC
intraclass correlation. Reliability: how closely a person's two results agree, from 0 (not at all) to 1 (exactly).
IRT
item response theory. A model of how the chance of a right answer depends on the person's ability and the question's difficulty.
M1 to M4
registered measures. The measures fixed in the pre-registration, for example M3, the pattern of errors.
MCI
mild cognitive impairment. Memory or thinking problems beyond normal ageing that do not yet stop independent living.
OSF
Open Science Framework. A public site where a study's plan is registered before any data, so it cannot be changed.
Q1 to Q48
open questions. Numbered questions the build could not answer by itself, each sent to the investigators or the clinicians.
RMSE
root mean square error. The typical size of an estimate's error.
SD
standard deviation. How spread out values are: the typical distance from the average.
SE
standard error. How far an estimate typically lands from the truth by chance.
